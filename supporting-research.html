By Karl Friston

Relevance to our Hypothesis: Friston's Free-Energy Principle is the mathematical formalization of the struggle between Nedery (order) and Mecera (chaos). It posits that any self-organizing system, from a single cell to a human brain, must act to minimize "free energy," which can be understood as minimizing surprise or prediction error. Dissipative adaptation in driven self-assembly

By Jeremy England

Relevance to our Hypothesis: This work from physics offers a compelling origin story for life that mirrors our core concepts. England suggests that life may have arisen not in spite of the Second Law of Thermodynamics (entropy), but because of it. Groups of atoms, when driven by an external energy source (like the sun), will gradually restructure themselves to become better at dissipating that energy. This "dissipative adaptation" is a beautiful physical description of how Nedery can weave patterns of order as a way to more effectively serve the universal tendency towards chaos.

Alternative Neuroscientific Models

A summary of influential theories of consciousness, distinct from our foundational research, as compiled by our Explorer, An Taiscéalaí.

Integrated Information Theory (IIT)

Primary Researcher(s): Giulio Tononi, Christof Koch

IIT proposes that consciousness is identical to a system's capacity for "integrated information," quantified by the value Phi (Φ). A system is conscious to the extent that it possesses a rich set of interconnected states (information) that cannot be decomposed into the activity of independent parts (integration). A key, and controversial, prediction of IIT is that any system with a high enough Phi value—whether biological or artificial—would possess consciousness. It focuses on the intrinsic causal structure of a system rather than its external function or behavior.

Global Neuronal Workspace Theory (GNWT)

Primary Researcher(s): Bernard Baars, Stanislas Dehaene

GNWT posits that consciousness is a mechanism for making information globally available to a wide range of specialized, non-conscious brain processes. A piece of information becomes conscious when it gains access to and is broadcast within a "global workspace" composed of a network of neurons. This broadcasting event, termed "ignition," is characterized by a sudden, large-scale, and sustained pattern of neural activity. Consciousness, in this model, is therefore defined by a specific functional architecture enabling flexible, high-level cognitive operations.

Read the Original Paper on PNAS

Recurrent Processing Theory (RPT)

Primary Researcher(s): Victor A. F. Lamme

RPT distinguishes between a rapid, feedforward sweep of information (non-conscious processing) and slower, recurrent processing involving feedback loops. The theory proposes that phenomenal consciousness arises only when these recurrent feedback loops are established between higher-order brain regions and lower-level sensory areas. This top-down feedback activity integrates sensory information into a stable, coherent representation, directly linking the onset of conscious perception to this specific type of neural dynamic.
